\section{Problem Awareness} \label{sec:problem-awareness}

This chapter establishes the meta-requirements that motivate the design principle space developed in Chapter~\ref{sec:design-principles}, through a hermeneutic literature review. Section~\ref{ssec:review-methodology} presents the review methodology, the resulting literature corpus, and the three thematic directions into which the corpus is organised. The subsequent sections discuss the corpus along these three directions: human-\ac{AI} interaction with \ac{GenAI} systems (Section~\ref{ssec:hai-genai}), the attribution of \ac{ToM} to \ac{AI} systems (Section~\ref{ssec:tom-attributions}), and explanations and mental model formation (Section~\ref{ssec:explanations-mental-model-formation}). The meta-requirements derived from these sections are synthesised in Section~\ref{ssec:meta-requirements}.

\subsection{Review Methodology and Corpus} \label{ssec:review-methodology}

The hermeneutic literature review is an interpretive, iterative approach to reviewing literature \parencite{Boell2014}. Rather than exhaustively searching a predefined corpus, the process begins with an initial set of works which is iteratively expanded through search and acquisition cycles \parencite{Boell2014}. During the search new works are discovered (e.g. by tracking citations) and then acquired if they contribute relevant additions to the corpus. The iterative movement between individual works and the evolving whole is the defining characteristic of the hermeneutic circle \parencite{Boell2014}. The process terminates when saturation is reached, i.e., when new sources cease to produce interpretively relevant additions to the corpus.

Due to the nascent state of the field the hermeneutic approach was uniquely suited for the review. This was reinforced by the interdisciplinary nature of the research topic, as well as practical constraints of a master's thesis scope. The starting corpus consisted of the four works introduced and discussed in Section~\ref{ssec:related-works}: \textcite{Weisz2025}, \textcite{Jussupow2021}, \textcite{Colombatto2025}, and \textcite{Ying2024}. Their relevance to this thesis had already been established, making them a natural starting point for the iterative expansion of the corpus.

The corpus was expanded primarily by tracing the citations of the starting corpus forward and backward \parencite[cf.][]{Boell2014}. Forward citation search was conducted using \href{https://scholar.google.com/}{Google Scholar}\footnote{https://scholar.google.com}, but yielded limited results due to the recency of the starting corpus, which is itself a reflection of the nascent state of the field. Backward citation search proved more productive: tracing the references of \textcite{Ying2024} led to \textcite{Shin2021}, whose references in turn led to \textcite{Arrieta2020} and \textcite{Miller2019}. \textcite{Amershi2019} was identified through \textcite{Weisz2025}'s related conference publication on the same design principle framework \parencite{Weisz2024}, which references it directly. \textcite{Hoffman2023} was identified through topical search rather than citation tracking.

Search was bounded thematically to topics relevant to the meta-requirements of this thesis, as established in the previous chapters. Additionally, a temporal boundary was set to 2017 onward, marking the introduction of the transformer architecture \parencite{Vaswani2017} as the technical inflection point that enabled modern \ac{GenAI} systems \parencite{Schneider2024}. Seminal works predating this boundary were included where they provide theoretical foundations that have not been superseded. Saturation was considered reached when new sources were found to either duplicate positions already represented in the corpus or focus on implementation details rather than conceptual or empirical contributions relevant to the meta-requirements.

The resulting corpus of nine works is organised into three thematic directions, which structure the remainder of this chapter:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \enquote{Human-\ac{AI} interaction with \ac{GenAI}}, covered in Section~\ref{ssec:hai-genai} and centred on \textcite{Weisz2025} and \textcite{Amershi2019}.
    \item \enquote{Theory of Mind attributions to \ac{AI} systems}, covered in Section~\ref{ssec:tom-attributions} and centred on \textcite{Jussupow2021}, \textcite{Colombatto2025}, and \textcite{Ying2024}.
    \item \enquote{Explanations and mental model formation}, covered in Section~\ref{ssec:explanations-mental-model-formation} and centred on \textcite{Miller2019}, \textcite{Shin2021}, \textcite{Hoffman2023}, and \textcite{Arrieta2020}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Human-AI Interaction with GenAI} \label{ssec:hai-genai}

Section~\ref{ssec:related-works} already established that \ac{GenAI} systems shift the user's role from \textit{command} to \textit{specification} \parencite{Weisz2025}, and that this shift renders existing \ac{HCI} frameworks insufficient for designing \ac{GenAI} interactions. This insufficiency is not merely asserted by \textcite{Weisz2025}; it can be illustrated concretely through \textcite{Amershi2019}, whose 18 guidelines for human-\ac{AI} interaction were distilled from over 150 design recommendations and validated through a multiphase evaluation, including a study with 49 \ac{HCI} practitioners testing the guidelines against 20 AI-infused products. Guidelines such as \enquote{make clear what the system can do} (G1), \enquote{make clear how well the system can do what it can do} (G2), and \enquote{make clear why the system did what it did} (G11) remain intuitively relevant to \ac{GenAI} systems. However, the guidelines and their underlying validation were developed against products that predate the rise of modern \ac{GenAI} systems, and consequently rest on an assumption of bounded, reproducible system behaviour that \ac{GenAI} systems violate by design \parencite{Schneider2024}. This is precisely the gap that motivates a separate set of design principles for \ac{GenAI} interaction rather than a direct extension of the existing guidelines \parencite{Weisz2025}.

Two of the resulting design principles are of particular relevance for the meta-requirements of this thesis: \textit{Design for Mental Models} and \textit{Design for Appropriate Trust and Reliance} \parencite{Weisz2025}. The remainder of this section examines the interaction challenges that motivate these two principles in more depth, focusing on aspects not already covered in Section~\ref{ssec:related-works}: the consequences of generative variability for mental model formation, and the conditions under which natural language interfaces invite anthropomorphism.

\subsubsection*{Non-Determinism and Mental Model Breakdown}

A defining property of \ac{GenAI} systems is \textit{generative variability}: the same input can produce different outputs across repeated invocations, and the quality of these outputs can vary considerably \parencite{Weisz2025}. Users accustomed to deterministic tools tend to interpret this variability as an error rather than as inherent system behaviour \parencite{Weisz2025}. This poses a particular challenge for mental model formation, as a model formed during an early interaction can break down once the system produces a different output for what the user perceives as an unchanged input.

\textcite{Gero2020} propose three categories of mental model content relevant to \ac{AI} systems: \textit{knowledge distribution}, describing the factual knowledge a system possesses; \textit{global behaviour}, describing general behavioural patterns of the system; and \textit{local behaviour}, describing the reasons for a specific output. Of these three categories, generative variability most directly undermines local behaviour, as users cannot reliably predict or reproduce why a system produced a specific output on a given occasion. This is also the category that explanations are best positioned to support, as an explanation of a specific output is, by definition, an account of local behaviour. The relationship between explanations and mental model formation is taken up in more depth in Section~\ref{ssec:explanations-mental-model-formation}.

The dependency between mental model accuracy and effective human-\ac{AI} collaboration is not specific to \ac{GenAI}. \textcite{Jussupow2021} show, in the context of a discriminative \ac{CAID} system, that physicians were only able to consistently accept correct and reject incorrect system advice when they held an accurate model of the system's capabilities (cf. Section~\ref{ssec:related-works}). This dependency plausibly extends to \ac{GenAI} systems as well, and arguably more urgently so: \ac{GenAI} outputs are harder to verify against an objective ground truth and less stable across repeated interactions than the outputs of the discriminative system studied by \textcite{Jussupow2021}.

\subsubsection*{Natural Language Interfaces and Anthropomorphism}

Natural language is a defining interface property of many \ac{GenAI} systems, lowering the barrier to entry and enabling the shift to specification-based interaction described above \parencite{Weisz2025}. The same property creates favourable conditions for spontaneous anthropomorphism: users attribute human-like qualities to a system without deliberate intent, simply as a consequence of interacting with it through natural language \parencite{Weisz2025}. \textcite{Weisz2025} situate this discussion under their principle of \textit{Design for Appropriate Trust and Reliance}, reflecting the close relationship between anthropomorphism and trust calibration.

The appropriate response to this tendency is contested. \textcite{Shneiderman2023} argue that anthropomorphic design should be avoided entirely, citing Microsoft's Clippy as a widely recognised example of anthropomorphic design that was not matched by corresponding system capability, resulting in user frustration rather than engagement. Despite disagreement on whether anthropomorphic design is desirable in principle, \textcite{Weisz2025} note a shared consensus that systems should not actively deceive users about their nature: \ac{AI} tools should be clearly identifiable as such, a concern that becomes more rather than less pressing as model capabilities improve.

Anthropomorphism is also relevant beyond interface design choices. Users do not only attribute human-like qualities to \ac{GenAI} systems incidentally; they apply the same folk-psychological concepts and reasoning processes used to explain human behaviour, including beliefs, desires, and intentions, to interpret \ac{AI} behaviour \parencite{Miller2019}. This connects the interaction-design concerns discussed in this section directly to the attribution of \ac{ToM} to \ac{AI} systems, which is discussed in Section~\ref{ssec:tom-attributions}.

\subsubsection*{Towards the Design Space}

The interaction challenges outlined above, the mismatch between existing \ac{HCI} guidelines and \ac{GenAI} properties, the breakdown of mental models under generative variability, and the conditions for anthropomorphism created by natural language interfaces, motivate the need for principled explanation design in \ac{GenAI} systems. They are also the reason \textcite{Weisz2025} treat \textit{Design for Mental Models} and \textit{Design for Appropriate Trust and Reliance} as two of six distinct design principles for \ac{GenAI}, rather than as a single undifferentiated concern. Sections~\ref{ssec:tom-attributions} and~\ref{ssec:explanations-mental-model-formation} take up these two threads in turn: who attributes what to \ac{GenAI} systems, and how explanations shape those attributions.
