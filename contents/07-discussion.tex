\section{Discussion} \label{sec:discussion}

This chapter synthesises the findings of the empirical evaluation and the design theory derived from it. Section~\ref{ssec:discussion-of-results} interprets the results against the theoretical framework established in Chapters~\ref{sec:fundamentals} and~\ref{sec:problem-awareness} and answers the research questions. Section~\ref{ssec:contributions} states the contributions of this thesis, and Section~\ref{ssec:limitations-future-work} reflects on the limitations of the research and outlines avenues for future work.

\subsection{Discussion of Results} \label{ssec:discussion-of-results}

The attribution of mental states to an interactive system is spontaneous and largely unavoidable. Users apply the same folk-psychological framework they use to explain human behaviour to artificial agents \parencite{Miller2019}, a disposition that requires neither language nor embodiment \parencite{Heider1944} and that extends to autonomous and intelligent systems in general \parencite{deGraaf2017, Muller2004}. Natural-language interaction creates particularly favourable conditions for it \parencite{Weisz2025}. This is what makes \ac{ToM} the route to the user's mental model of the system: users build that model \textit{by} attributing mental states to the system. The attributions are what is observable; the model is inferred from them. The results of this study add two qualifications to this picture. Attribution is not only voiced but enacted, and it is selective in what it covers.

Participants attributed beliefs, desires, and intentions to the chatbot, but not uniformly across the three components. \textit{Beliefs} were attributed most explicitly, concentrated in the \textit{Mental Model \& Updates} dimension, and typically surfaced when participants detected a discrepancy between the system's assumptions and the information given in the task. \textit{Desires} were attributed more indirectly, carried by how participants repeated the system's guidance and by what their subsequent actions implied about the goals they ascribed to it. \textit{Intention} attribution was marginal. Measured against the four signatures of meta-representation introduced in Section~\ref{sssec:theory-of-mind} \parencite{Rakoczy2022}, belief attribution reaches three. Participants regularly invoked \textit{difference} and \textit{incompatibility} to establish a conflict between their own representation of the task and the system's. \textit{Misrepresentation} is reached through the pattern code \textit{attributing LLM's confusion to own action}, which traces a false representation held by the system back to the participant's own input; the evidence here is thin, but consistent. \textit{Aspectuality} was not reached.

The uneven coverage is a property of the analytic lens as much as of the phenomenon. Process-focused coding surfaces those attributions that carried consequences the participant had to act on, whereas attributions consumed silently while reading leave no process trace and were absorbed into the restatement of content. This predicts exactly the pattern observed: a belief attribution carries consequences, because a detected discrepancy forces a decision, and therefore surfaces; a desire attribution is mostly acted on without needing to be resolved; an intention attribution rarely required any action at all. The resulting coverage is good for belief, indirect for desire, and marginal for intention. This is carried forward as a limitation of the lens in Section~\ref{ssec:limitations-future-work}. The corresponding finding is that attribution can be inferred from process-coded behaviour without ever being voiced.

Attribution runs across the whole interaction and is only rarely made explicit. The interaction repeatedly follows the same pattern: participants decide whether and how to engage the system (\textit{LLM Reliance Decisions}), exchange information with it (\textit{Information Exchange}), and appraise what they receive (\textit{Mental Model \& Updates}). Attribution is not confined to the statements coded under \textit{LLM Attributions}. Offloading decisions and the uptake of guidance are behavioural enactments of an attributed capability belief: to delegate a step is to act on a belief about what the system is able to do.

The direction of that default is consistent. Of the 96 appraisal statements defined in Section~\ref{ssec:initial-design-principles}, seven fall under \textit{Identifying \& Correcting LLM Errors}, approximately 7\%. Constructing the analogous set for the participants' appraisal of their own work, 69 of 206 statements fall under \textit{Errors \& Correction (Self)}, approximately 34\%. Discrepancies between the participant's representation and the system's were therefore resolved in the system's favour roughly five times more often than against it, conditional on appraisal taking place rather than on mere exposure. In the terms of \textcite{Jussupow2021}, the belief conflict is resolved in favour of the system by default. One confound must be stated: no per-task correctness data were recorded for the system, so a genuinely lower system error rate cannot be excluded. The conclusion holds under either reading. Either participants held a genuine competence default, or they held a default that happened to be calibrated by accident rather than by verification. In neither case is the default the product of validation. This should not be called trust: no trust construct was measured in this study, and self-reported trust and behavioural reliance are known to come apart \parencite[cf.][]{Colombatto2025}.

Where the collaboration broke down, the failure most often traced back to the \textit{knowledge distribution} between user and system \parencite{Gero2020}: participants held a misrepresentation of what the system knows. Three of the design principles address this locus directly. \textbf{DP3} and \textbf{DP5} improve the communication of the system's knowledge distribution to the user, \textbf{DP1} shapes the system's \textit{global behaviour}, and \textbf{DP-T1} addresses its \textit{local behaviour}. Beyond these, \textbf{DP7} and \textbf{DP-T3} invert the direction of interest, from the user's model of the system to the system's model of the user. This is precisely what \ac{MToM} advocates \parencite{Wang2021, Wang2024a}, and it constitutes an extension of \textcite{Gero2020}'s categories, which are formulated unidirectionally: the same three categories can be applied in the opposite direction.

Participants also used anthropomorphic vocabulary when referring to the chatbot, recurring across all coded participants but varying considerably in density. It is carried by volitional and epistemic predicates, e.g., \enquote{er will die ganze Zeit, dass ich \ldots} [he wants me to \ldots all the time] (ID: \texttt{03.020.2}) and \enquote{das kann er ja gar nicht wissen} [he cannot possibly know that] (ID: \texttt{11.022.4}), and by social acts directed at the system, e.g., \enquote{Vielen Dank, Chatbot} [thank you, chatbot] (ID: \texttt{02.025.10}). Agent framing alternates with machine framing within the same participant, e.g., \enquote{es scheint die KI dann zu hängen} [the AI then seems to hang] (ID: \texttt{08.034.5}). German grammatical gender makes pronominal reference uninformative here, as \enquote{er} is simply the pronoun for \enquote{der Chatbot}, so the claim rests on the predicates and social acts alone. This observation was made post hoc rather than coded and is therefore anecdotal.

The quantitative layer serves as triangulation only. \textbf{Frustration} ($p=.005$) and \textbf{Perceived Usefulness} ($p=.095$) differ significantly between the groups at $\alpha=0.1$, and the remaining workload constructs are directionally consistent with the hypothesised effect (cf. Section~\ref{sssec:gap-analysis}).

\textbf{RQ1} asks which mental states \ac{LLM}-users attribute to \acp{LLM} in collaborative problem-solving scenarios, and what these attributions reveal about their mental model of the system. Beliefs and desires are attributed, while intention attribution is limited. Attribution is continuous across the interaction cycle and mostly enacted rather than voiced, carried by offloading and guidance uptake, and becoming explicit primarily at points of breakdown. The default is an attribution of competence to the system. Coverage across the three components is uneven: belief is articulated at those breakdowns and reaches three of the four signatures of meta-representation, desire is widespread but indirect, and intention is marginal. This asymmetry is partly a property of the instrument and partly of the phenomenon, as set out in the observability conditions above. What this reveals about the mental model is that it is held without being stated, acted on rather than articulated, and revised only when an expectation about system behaviour is violated.

\textbf{RQ2} asks whether providing explanations for \ac{LLM} outputs influences users' attribution of mental states, and if so in what direction and on which dimensions of attribution. A dimension-level between-group comparison was ruled out in Section~\ref{ssec:initial-design-principles} by the \textit{Organisation} control. The sole defensible finding is one-sided: explanations increased the share of appraisal moves that reached deliberate validation, with heuristic uptake at 17.4\% for Group E against 36.0\% for Group B. Because these are within-group ratios, they are unaffected by the verbosity difference that the \textit{Organisation} control exposes.

\textbf{RQ3} asks which design principles can be derived for \ac{XAI} in \ac{GenAI} systems to support accurate mental models of \ac{LLM}-based systems. It is answered by the \acp{MR}, \acp{DR}, and \textbf{DP1}~--~\textbf{DP7} shown in Figure~\ref{fig:mr-dr-dp-mapping-revised}, which are derived from the findings for \textbf{RQ1} and \textbf{RQ2} and evaluated through expert interviews. The capability-expectation cluster belongs here as well: \textbf{DP2} and \textbf{DP6} address the alignment of capability expectations empirically, and \textbf{DP-T5} complements them from theory, building on G1 and G2 by \textcite{Amershi2019} and on the error-boundary argument of \textcite{Bansal2019}. Part of the design space remains uncovered by the empirically derived principles, as no principle addresses explanations as a mechanism and \textbf{DR2} is addressed one-sidedly. \textbf{DP-T1}~--~\textbf{DP-T5} close that space (Figure~\ref{fig:mr-dr-dp-mapping-final}), but they are derived from literature rather than from \textbf{RQ1} and \textbf{RQ2} and remain unevaluated. This boundary is kept visible throughout.

Users form and hold a mental model of \ac{LLM}-based systems, without explicitly stating it. They act based on this mental model and only revise it when their expectations of system behaviour are violated. In this study, such discrepancies were resolved in the system's favour almost five times more often than against it. Consequently, the design goal is not only to help users form an accurate mental model, but also to expose the existing one to challenge in situations where it would otherwise go unexamined.

\subsection{Contributions} \label{ssec:contributions}

The design principles developed in this thesis guide the design of systems towards both of these goals. The formation of an accurate mental model is supported by \textbf{DP2} (system limitations), \textbf{DP3} (information provenance), \textbf{DP5} (model assumptions), \textbf{DP-T1} (local behaviour), \textbf{DP-T2} (unfaithful explanations), and \textbf{DP-T5} (system capabilities). Making the user's existing mental model visible and challengeable is served by \textbf{DP4}, which surfaces conflicts the user would otherwise not see, \textbf{DP6}, which forces users to articulate their expectations before querying, \textbf{DP-T3}, which places a challenge at the moment the model is already in question, and \textbf{DP-T4}, which forces fact-based deliberation before an output is dismissed. The second goal was not anticipated by the \acp{MR} and emerged from the analysis; it is reported here as a finding of this thesis rather than as a requirement it set out to satisfy.

The second contribution concerns a failure mode that inverts a known one. \textcite{Jussupow2021} describe \textit{AI-based confirmation}, in which the physician anchors on the system's advice before an independent frame has formed. The data of this study show the inverse: the \textit{system} anchors on the user's erroneous input and endorses it, most clearly when the chatbot adopted the sign error introduced by P08 \parencite[cf.][]{Sharma2024}, which the participant later commented on explicitly (ID: \texttt{08.048.6}). The corpus studied by \textcite{Jussupow2021} has no room for this failure mode, as a discriminative \ac{CAID} system issuing binary advice cannot adopt a user's premise; a conversational \ac{GenAI} system can. A single instance is sufficient as an existence proof, while its generalisation and its importance across contexts remain open. \textbf{DP4} is the counter-mechanism, and two experts independently noted during the evaluation that surfacing conflicts works against the sycophantic tendencies of \acp{LLM}.

The third contribution extends \textcite{Gero2020}'s categories bidirectionally. \textit{Knowledge distribution} is the locus of the collaboration failures observed in this study, addressed by \textbf{DP3} and \textbf{DP5}, with \textbf{DP1} addressing \textit{global behaviour} and \textbf{DP-T1} \textit{local behaviour}. Beyond this, \textbf{DP7} and \textbf{DP-T3} extend the direction of interest to the system's model of the user's mental state. This is explicitly an extension, as \textcite{Gero2020} formulate the categories unidirectionally, and it is routed through \ac{MToM} \parencite{Wang2021, Wang2024a}.

Faithfulness delimits what parts of this design theory can be relied upon. \ac{CoT} explanations are not guaranteed to reflect the process that produced an output \parencite{Lyu2023}, and guaranteeing faithfulness remains unsolved \parencite{Tutek2025}; E03 raised the same concern independently during the evaluation. Every principle whose mechanism depends on the accuracy of an explanation or of a system self-report inherits this exposure. Its practical impact is limited by the dual design goals established above: even an unfaithful explanation can surface and challenge an existing mental model, which is the second goal, whereas only a faithful one can reliably serve the first. \textbf{DP1}, \textbf{DP6}, and \textbf{DP7} are unaffected, as none of them relies on explanations or on self-reported data. Guaranteed faithfulness would nevertheless improve the effectiveness of the design theory as a whole.

Three practical implications follow for practitioners. First, \textbf{the availability of an explanation does not guarantee its consumption}. The \ac{CoT} was rendered collapsed and click-to-expand (Section~\ref{sssec:model-selection}), and only two pattern codes touch it at all. The evidence is partial, as task difficulty and silent reading compete as explanations for the same observation (Section~\ref{sssec:gap-analysis}), but the risk is worth stating: an explanation placed behind an interaction step may simply not be read. Second, \textbf{out-of-scope requests should be rejected clearly}. The system was instructed not to solve the problems (\textbf{CR1}, Section~\ref{sssec:model-selection}), yet participants still requested solutions, e.g., \enquote{löse es [Gleichungssystem] mir auf und berechne die lösungen} [solve it for me and calculate the solutions] (ID: \texttt{03.024.1}). A refusal should be paired with a disclosure of what the system can do (\textbf{DP2} and \textbf{DP-T5}), or it reads as a failure rather than as a boundary. Third, \textbf{friction is a budget}. \textbf{DR4} and \textbf{DR5} stand in deliberate tension (Section~\ref{ssec:meta-requirements}), and the experts raised habituation as a concern for \textbf{DP6}, which applies equally to \textbf{DP-T4}. Friction should be spent only where \textbf{DR4}'s consequentiality test is met, that is where an action is irreversible, costly to correct, or potentially harmful \parencite[cf.][]{Buinca2021}.

\subsection{Limitations \& Future Work} \label{ssec:limitations-future-work}

The structural cause of the gap identified for \textbf{RQ2} is the retrospective nature of the analysis. The study was designed to investigate cognition and metacognition, not mental model formation or \ac{ToM} attribution. No \ac{ToM} instrument was administered: the scales used by \textcite{Colombatto2025} and \textcite{Gray2007} and a behavioural reliance measure were never in scope, because the questions they answer were not the questions the study was built to answer. This limitation is inherent to the reflective approach prescribed by the method for design principle development \parencite{Moeller2020} and subsumes the dimension-level problem reported for \textbf{RQ2}.

The reach of the analytical lens is bounded in a second way. The BDI frame was applied to finished pattern codes rather than built into the coding scheme, which applied process and in vivo coding first \parencite{Saldana2015}. Coverage is therefore uneven: belief is reached well, desire only where it coincided with a participant's process, and intention barely at all. This is a question of analytic coverage, not of measurement validity, as no validated measure of attribution is claimed. The observability conditions given in Section~\ref{ssec:discussion-of-results} explain which attributions the lens can reach and which it cannot, so the boundary of the picture is known rather than unknown. Beyond this, the mental models themselves were never observed. What is reported is \textcite{Norman1983}'s fourth element: the researcher's reconstruction of the user's model from the attribution behaviour available.

Two limitations arise from the execution of the study. The task difficulty fell below the threshold at which an explanation becomes necessary, as evidenced by 32 of 36 tasks being solved correctly, which made engaging with the explanation optional rather than necessary. This affects the statistical power of the comparison rather than the design concept, and was identifiable only after execution. Further, the manipulation is not isolable: instructing the model to produce a chain of thought changes both the content of its responses \parencite{Wei2022, Kojima2022} and whether a trace is displayed at all, so the between-group evidence speaks only weakly to the effect of \textit{displaying} an explanation.

The analysis was conducted by a single researcher, so no intercoder reliability, negotiated agreement, or member checking was possible, contrary to the team-based assumption underlying \textcite{Gioia2013}. This exposure extends across the coding, the application of the BDI frame, the construction of the data structure, the abductive derivation of the design principles, and the completion of the evaluation forms. The hermeneutic literature review carries the same exposure \parencite{Boell2014}. Finally, the study consisted of a single session per participant. \textcite{Amershi2019}'s \enquote{over time} category is therefore unobservable, and the habituation concern the experts raised for \textbf{DP6} cannot be answered with these data.

Five directions for future work follow directly from these limitations.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Instantiate and evaluate the full design theory}, including \textbf{DP-T1}~--~\textbf{DP-T5}. Splitting the instantiation across several artefacts rather than building one composite system would allow individual mechanisms to be isolated, and reliance calibration should be measured in the manner of \textcite{Jussupow2021} rather than through self-reported trust \parencite{Colombatto2025}.
    \item \textbf{Prospective attribution coding}, building the BDI frame into the coding scheme so that desire and intention become reachable by design, paired with a think-aloud protocol that prompts participants for their expectations of the system.
    \item \textbf{Longitudinal design}, with repeated sessions across several weeks, targeting habituation to friction (\textbf{DP6}, \textbf{DP-T4}), calibration drift, and the stability of attribution over time.
    \item \textbf{\ac{ToM}-appropriate constructs}, administering the scales of \textcite{Colombatto2025} and \textcite{Gray2007} alongside a behavioural reliance measure, at a sample size that supports inferential testing.
    \item \textbf{Replication isolating display}, generating a chain of thought in both conditions but displaying it in only one, at a task difficulty above the explanation-need threshold.
\end{itemize}
