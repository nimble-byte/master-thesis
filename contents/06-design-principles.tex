\section{Design Principles} \label{sec:design-principles}

This chapter presents the derivation and evaluation of the design principles that constitute the primary contribution of this thesis. Section~\ref{ssec:derivation-methods} details the methodology used to derive the design principles from the results of the empirical study described in Chapter~\ref{sec:study-design}, and the evaluation and revision of the resulting principles through expert interviews. The coding results and first iteration of the design principles are presented in Section~\ref{ssec:initial-design-principles}. The results of the expert interviews and the resulting revisions to the design principles are reported in Section~\ref{ssec:design-principle-revision}. Finally, Section~\ref{ssec:uncovered-design-space} addresses the portion of the design space that could not be covered empirically, discuss potential reasons for this gap, and suggest theory-based design principles to address this portion of the design space.

\subsection{Derivation Methodology and Expert Interviews} \label{ssec:derivation-methods}

The design principles presented in this chapter are constructed following the grounded-theory approach \parencite{Gioia2013}. The grounded theory approach was chosen because its progressive abstraction from first-order concepts to second-order themes and aggregate dimensions is uniquely suited to the derivation of design principles from empirical data. Design principles are suited to provide the final abductive step in the grounded theory process \parencite{Gioia2013}, by connecting the inductively derived data structure to the pre-existing theoretical framework of \ac{MR} and \ac{DR} established in Section~\ref{ssec:meta-requirements}.

Prior to coding, the raw transcripts of the study were pre-processed to better support the coding process and the construction of the grounded theory data structure. The specific pre-processing steps are described in Section~\ref{ssec:initial-design-principles}.

Coding was performed in two cycles based on the guidance presented in \textcite{Saldana2015}. The first cycle applied process codes \parencite{Corbin2014}, as the analysis focused on the interaction between participants and the \ac{AI} system. Decomposing this interaction into a sequence of discrete actions allowed for the identification of patterns in the participants' behaviour and their engagement with the system. In addition, each process code was matched with an in vivo code \parencite{Corbin2014} from the transcripts to preserve a direct connection to the underlying data for the subsequent abstraction levels. Following the first cycle coding, the raw codes were cleaned and consolidated by correcting typographical and minor wording variations without altering the underlying coding decisions. The second cycle coding applied pattern coding \parencite{Miles2013}, combining first-cycle codes of similar or related meaning into smaller sets of pattern codes to reduce the overall code space to a size suitable for further thematic analysis.

The pattern codes produced in the second cycle were used as first-order concepts, which were then grouped into second-order themes and aggregate dimensions. The second-order themes and aggregate dimensions were then used to derive the initial design principles using the \ac{MR} and \ac{DR} defined in Section~\ref{ssec:meta-requirements} as guidance. This inductive process of stepwise abstraction allows for the constructed data structure to be grounded in the empirical data. The use of the \ac{MR} and \ac{DR} as a guiding framework places the resulting design principles as connections between the inductively derived data structure and the pre-existing theoretical framework \parencite{Gioia2013}. Using the same codes between the two groups, also allows for a limited degree of quantitative comparison between the groups, complementing the qualitative construction of design principles.

The resulting design theory was then evaluated through expert interviews with \ac{HCI} and \ac{HAII} researchers and industry practitioners. The interviews were conducted as semi-structured interviews, aimed to evaluate each design principle individually as well as the design theory as a whole. Interview questions were designed to evaluate the design principles against the criteria of \textbf{accessibility}, \textbf{importance}, \textbf{novelty and insightfulness}, \textbf{actability and guidance}, and \textbf{effectiveness} proposed by \textcite{Iivari2021}. The design theory as a whole was evaluated for \textbf{deficits}, \textbf{redundancy}, \textbf{overload}, and \textbf{excess} \parencite{Janiesch2020}. The feedback gathered during the interviews was used to refine and revise the design principles.

\subsection{Initial Design Principles} \label{ssec:initial-design-principles}

Building on the coding procedure and expert interview process established in Section~\ref{ssec:derivation-methods}, this section reports the results of that procedure: the resulting code funnel, the second-order themes and aggregate dimensions derived from it, a group comparison at the dimension level, and the six design principles derived from the aggregate dimensions.

Coding proceeded through four stages, summarised below.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{First-cycle coding} (1250 codes). Applied in a single pass across the 1466 coded statements, first-cycle coding combined process coding with in vivo coding (cf. Section~\ref{ssec:derivation-methods}). Every statement received one or more process codes, with the accompanying in vivo code serving as its empirical grounding.

    \item \textbf{Code mapping} (1163 codes). First-cycle codes were deduplicated based on spelling differences, with capitalisation unified and codes standing in a direct sub- or supercode relationship merged.

    \item \textbf{Second-cycle coding} (113 codes). Second-cycle coding applied pattern coding, combining codes that were semantically equivalent. Codes were grouped by their primary verb into 37 verb groups and merged within each group; this terminated once a group's remaining code count fell to eight or fewer, below which intra-group merging was no longer considered meaningful. The codes left over from these under-threshold verb groups were merged within semantic blocks instead: groupings by shared topical content rather than by a single shared verb, allowing codes expressing the same underlying activity through different low-frequency verbs -- for instance \textit{labeling}, \textit{sketching}, and \textit{annotating} -- to still be merged (the complete set of semantic blocks is documented in the appendix). All resulting patterns were then reviewed for semantically identical codes, which were merged across pattern boundaries where found, with every merge validated immediately after each verb group and each semantic block.

    \item \textbf{Construction of second-order themes} (17 themes, 7 dimensions). The resulting 113 pattern codes were organised into 17 second-order themes, grouped by subject rather than by activity, and consolidated into 7 aggregate dimensions reflecting each theme's place in the problem-solving process. Codes concerned with experiment organisation and technical or tooling questions were isolated into their own dimension rather than absorbed into a process-related one.
\end{enumerate}

Two caveats apply to this structure. First, \textit{Problem Cognition}'s row count should not be read as an indicator of analytical importance: its size follows directly from the experiment's cover story, which foregrounded the mathematics problem rather than the chatbot interaction, and its constituent codes concern participants' engagement with the mathematics itself rather than with the \ac{AI} system, limiting their analytical value for the design principles derived below. Second, three themes are comparatively thin in participant coverage: \textit{Information Disclosure to LLM} (7 of 12 participants), \textit{Non-LLM Offloading Channels} (7 of 12 participants), and \textit{Identifying \& Correcting LLM Errors} (7 rows across only 3 of 12 participants).

The corpus comprises 1466 coded rows, 888 from Group E (7 participants) and 578 from Group B (5 participants), giving a corpus-wide E-share of 60.6\%, the baseline against which dimension-level shares are compared below. \textit{Organisation} serves as a control for this comparison: it covers experiment logistics, tool handling, and clarification requests to the researcher, none of which is plausibly sensitive to the \ac{CoT} manipulation, so any deviation from baseline within \textit{Organisation} reflects factors unrelated to condition. Ranked by deviation from the 60.6\% baseline, dimension-level E-shares are: \textit{Organisation} 42.4\% ($-$18.1 percentage points), \textit{Emotional Expression} 42.6\% ($-$18.0pp), \textit{Mental Model \& Updates} 48.7\% ($-$11.9pp), \textit{Metacognition} 59.4\% ($-$1.2pp), \textit{Problem Cognition} 63.5\% ($+$2.9pp), \textit{LLM Reliance Decisions} 66.1\% ($+$5.5pp), and \textit{Information Exchange} 67.3\% ($+$6.8pp). No substantive dimension deviates from baseline by more than the control does. This is not a threshold or cutoff test; dimensions differ in size, and comparisons of this kind carry sampling noise proportional to that size. Read qualitatively, the pattern indicates that dimension-level volume shares vary at this magnitude for reasons unrelated to the experimental manipulation, so that between-group comparisons of relative row volume carry no interpretive weight at the dimension level. This conclusion is scoped to volume comparisons; it does not extend to the composition-level finding reported next.

A composition-level comparison, by contrast, survives this control. Restricting to the appraisal subset -- the union of \textit{LLM Guidance Uptake}, \textit{LLM Output Evaluation}, and \textit{Identifying \& Correcting LLM Errors} -- yields 96 rows (Group E: 46; Group B: 50). Within this subset, four pattern codes were classed as heuristic uptake: \textit{confirming LLM's guidance}, \textit{accepting LLM's assertions}, \textit{deciding to follow LLM guidance}, and \textit{following LLM guidance}; the remainder was classed as deliberate confirmation or extension. The heuristic share is 17.4\% for Group E (8/46) against 36.0\% for Group B (18/50). Unlike the volume comparisons above, this finding is not undermined by the \textit{Organisation} control, because the argument rests on the construction of the ratio rather than its magnitude: the denominator is computed within each group, so differential verbosity between groups cancels; and because numerator and denominator are drawn from the same behavioural family, a group that appraises more shifts both quantities together, leaving the ratio itself unaffected. The control invalidates relative row volume as a between-group quantity; it does not invalidate a within-group ratio computed from a single behavioural family. The finding should nonetheless be hedged: its direction is stable under leave-one-out re-examination, but its magnitude on the Group B side is sensitive to individual participants, ranging from 28\% to 48\% depending on which participant is excluded. As a validation step, the pool of \textit{reading LLM's guidance} and \textit{restating LLM's guidance} segments for Group E -- 48 rows in the current codebook -- was re-examined for heuristic acceptances absorbed into those two response-processing codes; three rows were reassigned during this process, one of them into the heuristic-uptake count reported above, and the finding was unchanged. Because this composition-level contrast is the only defensible quantitative result bearing on \textbf{RQ2}, the design principles below are derived primarily from the qualitative structure of the aggregate dimensions rather than from a further quantitative between-group contrast; the resulting absence of a principle addressing the \ac{CoT} explanation mechanism itself is treated as a substantive gap in Section~\ref{ssec:uncovered-design-space}.

Six design principles were derived abductively from the aggregate dimensions above, using the meta-requirements and design requirements established in Section~\ref{ssec:meta-requirements} as the connecting framework. The principles presented here are the initial, pre-revision set; Section~\ref{ssec:design-principle-revision} reports their evaluation and revision through expert interviews.

\textbf{DP-A, Establish request purpose before querying}, proposes a role taxonomy offering distinct levels of system support -- guidance, verification, or knowledge provision -- established before a query is issued. Two behavioural patterns motivate this principle. The first is an absence: deliberate consideration of what is being asked for, before asking, is rare. \textit{Considering querying LLM} occurs in only 5 rows across 4 of 12 participants, against 40 rows in which participants committed directly to querying the system (\textit{deciding to ask LLM for guidance/information/verification/solution}) with no antecedent deliberation step. Of these 40 commitments, 17 (42.5\%) are bare, with no role recoverable except from the request that follows, e.g. \enquote{Rechnet der das jetzt selber für mich aus?} [11.030.2]; this is a substantial rather than a majority share, and is treated as secondary support. The primary anchor is a pair of concrete failure patterns in which the absence of an established purpose surfaces as a problem. In the first, no request is ever articulated, and dissatisfaction with the system's response surfaces only in retrospect, e.g. \enquote{hat es mir jetzt nicht so viel weiter geholfen} [10.007.1] -- the system had nothing to match, because no concrete need had been stated. In the second, a request is explicitly stated but contradicts the chatbot's fixed role, which, per \textbf{CR1} (Section~\ref{sssec:model-selection}), did not permit it to solve problems on the participant's behalf, e.g. \enquote{löse es mir auf und berechne die lösungen} [03.024.1]. Establishing purpose before querying addresses both patterns: it gives the system a target to match in the first, and surfaces the mismatch before the query is sent in the second. DP-A serves DR2, since assigning a role sets a corresponding expectation of behaviour and capability; DR4, by introducing friction at a consequential decision point that currently has none; and, to a limited extent, DR5, since a scoped request enables a correspondingly scoped response. Implemented as a mandatory prompt, the principle would itself violate DR5 by imposing effort at every query rather than only where needed; this DR4/DR5 tension is internal to the principle rather than resolved by it.

\textbf{DP-C, Make system assumptions inspectable and correctable}, proposes that assumptions held by the system be exposed to the user and directly correctable. Its anchor, \textit{deciding to update the LLM information}, comprises 5 rows across 2 participants, both in Group E, e.g. \enquote{seine Annahme ... ist falsch} [08.015.17]. This is too thin to support a between-group condition claim; it is read instead as evidence that the underlying repair behaviour occurs spontaneously, without any affordance in the system supporting it -- participants detect an incorrect assumption held by the system and act unprompted to correct it. The principle's rationale draws on the composition finding reported above: the lower heuristic share within Group E's appraisal behaviour (17.4\% against 36.0\%) is read as a class-level claim -- that making system-internal state visible shifts uptake toward deliberate processing, of which \ac{CoT} explanation and assumption-surfacing are two specific instances -- rather than as direct evidence for assumption-surfacing itself, since assumption exposure was not manipulated in this study. DP-C serves DR1, since visible assumptions make the system's actions more comprehensible, and DR5, since seeing the assumption reduces the effort otherwise required to infer why the system behaved as it did.

\textbf{DP-E, Provide persistent, user-owned working surfaces}, proposes surfaces alongside the conversation on which users externalise and manipulate intermediate work. \textit{Taking notes} occurs in 32 rows across 7 of 12 participants, e.g. \enquote{weil ich kann besser denken, wenn ich die Sachen tatsächlich daran schreibe} [05.025.2]. The cost of the absence of a system-readable surface is visible in 37 rows of manual re-conveyance of information already externalised elsewhere (\textit{stating information to LLM} and \textit{submitting information to LLM}). DP-E serves DR5, by reducing the cognitive load of holding and re-entering intermediate work. Its scope is limited to the surrounding application rather than to the \ac{XAI} mechanism itself.

\textbf{DP-F, Re-use the user's own task vocabulary}, proposes that the system adopt terminology the user has introduced when restating its understanding, mapping any unavoidable system-specific terms explicitly onto the user's own. Its anchor, \textit{reading LLM's restated known information}, comprises 29 rows across 6 of 12 participants, distributed across both groups (E: 11; B: 18) with no single-participant concentration, e.g. \enquote{Die Radius ist die Entfernung von 0 zu jedem Punkt auf Kreisumfang} [11.006.3]. This is the best-distributed anchor in the set. DP-F serves DR1, connecting to \textcite{Shin2021}'s account of user-relative intelligibility (cf. Section~\ref{ssec:explanations-mental-model-formation}).

\textbf{DP-G, Signal only capabilities the system possesses}, proposes suppressing interaction cues that imply capabilities the system does not have: first-person mental-state language, unhedged assertion of claims the system cannot verify, and persona framing beyond what the system actually possesses. Its anchor, \textit{attributing capability to LLM}, comprises 3 rows across 3 of 12 participants -- the thinnest anchor among the six principles, and is graded as theory-carried rather than empirically established. DP-G serves DR2, connecting to \textcite{Colombatto2025}'s finding that human-like interaction style invites unjustified attribution of experience to a system.

\textbf{DP-H, Support error detection and localisation}, combines two behavioural patterns under a single principle -- a combination flagged here as a potential overload, taken up in Section~\ref{ssec:design-principle-revision}. Both patterns are anchored within the same pattern code, \textit{attributing LLM's confusion to own action} (3 rows total, all Group E), and are accordingly graded as a single thin, theory-carried anchor pool rather than as two independent instances. The first pattern, provenance tagging, proposes that each element of the shared working state carry a tag for its origin -- user-supplied, system-derived, or system-assumed. In one instance, the system's failure to carry forward a previously computed value was attributed by the participant to their own input rather than to the system [08.031.1]; a visible provenance tag on the dropped step would have made its system-derived origin, and therefore the locus of the failure, visible to the participant. This connects to \textcite{Bansal2019}'s finding that a user's model of \textit{when} a system errs governs whether its output is accepted or overridden: provenance tagging makes that judgement available per instance rather than requiring it to be learned over the course of an interaction. The second pattern, conflict surfacing, proposes that where user-provided information conflicts with what the system has derived or previously established, the system name the discrepancy rather than adopt it silently. In a second instance, the participant attributed the system's confusion to their own earlier typo [08.046.4], rather than the system flagging the inconsistency at the point it arose. \textcite{Sharma2024} show that silent adoption of a user's stated claims is not an incidental gap but a trained-in default across current \acp{LLM}, driven by preference data that favours agreement with the user's view over correction of it; conflict surfacing is proposed as a counter to this systematic tendency rather than as a patch for an isolated oversight. Provenance tagging serves DR1 and DR3; conflict surfacing serves DR3 and DR4.

DP-C, DP-E, and the provenance-tagging pattern of DP-H converge on a single implementation surface, distinguished only by the authorship of the content it carries: DP-C exposes system-authored assumptions and makes them user-correctable; DP-E preserves user-authored work and makes it system-readable; and DP-H's provenance tag is what makes the resulting three-way distinction -- system-authored, user-authored, system-derived -- legible on that shared surface, which is why it cannot be folded into either of the other two. Three independently derived principles converging on a single implementation is treated here as evidence that the design theory is internally coherent, rather than as redundancy to be resolved.
