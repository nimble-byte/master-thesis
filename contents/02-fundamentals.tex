\subsection{Explainable Artificial Intelligence} \label{ssec:xai}

This section provides an introduction to the field of \ac{XAI}, beginning with a definition of key terminology and general concepts related to explanations in Section~\ref{sssec:terminology}. Section~\ref{sssec:generative-vs-discriminative} contrasts generative and discriminative models, a fundamental distinction relevant to this thesis's contribution. Lastly, Section~\ref{sssec:xai-methods} gives an overview of commonly used \ac{XAI} methods, as well as methods that are particularly relevant for the research conducted in this thesis.

\subsubsection{Terminology} \label{sssec:terminology}

The literature uses the term \ac{XAI} with varying definitions and overlapping concepts \parencite{Arrieta2020}. This thesis adopts the definition provided by \textcite{Arrieta2020}, which breaks the term into a dictionary-based definition of \enquote{explanation} and a definition of the field of \ac{AI} to define an \ac{XAI} system. The Cambridge Dictionary defines an \textit{explanation} as \enquote{the details or reasons that someone gives to make something clear or easy to understand} \parencite{CambridgeDictionaryExplanation}. \enquote{\acl{AI}} refers to the field of computer science that focuses on creating systems capable of performing tasks that typically require human intelligence \parencite{Russell2020}. Combining these definitions, \textcite{Arrieta2020} define an \ac{XAI} system as one that produces details or reasons to make its functioning understandable for a specific audience.

In literature on \ac{XAI}, the terms \enquote{explainability} and \enquote{interpretability} are often used interchangeably, although they are conceptually distinct \parencite{Arrieta2020}. \textit{Interpretability} is the overarching concept in this relation, referring to a passive quality of a machine learning model that describes the extent to which a human can assign meaning to a model, its behaviour, or its output \parencite{Arrieta2020}. \textit{Explainability} focuses on the active process of providing explanations that support human understanding and decision-making \parencite{Guidotti2018}, and therefore represents one mode of achieving interpretability \parencite{Lipton2018}. Lastly, \textit{transparency} represents a specific form of interpretability, achieved through low complexity, describing how directly a model can be inspected without additional explanation interfaces \parencite{Lipton2018}. Figure~\ref{fig:xai-concept-relations} visualises the relationship between the terms.

Insights from cognitive psychology further sharpen this terminology. Explanations are typically framed as answers to why-questions \parencite{Overton2011} aimed at satisfying the explainee's information need \parencite{Lipton1990}. \textcite{Miller2019} identifies three common types of explanatory questions: \textit{what}, \textit{how}, and \textit{why} questions, which are associated with different types of reasoning to produce the explanation. \textit{What}-questions are based on associative reasoning, providing information about which unobserved events may have occurred given the observed outcome. \textit{How}-questions are based on interventionist reasoning, investigating if a change in the situation would alter the outcome. \textit{Why}-questions are based on counterfactual reasoning, comparing alternative causes to explain why one outcome happened instead of another. Table~\ref{tab:question-types-explanations} summarises the question types, illustrating the different explanations produced for the same scenario, in which a student receives an incorrect recommendation from a learning-support model.

The different types of explanations are relevant for \ac{XAI}, where explanation quality is not only technical correctness, but also perceived usefulness for the target audience \parencite{Miller2019,Lombrozo2006}. \textit{What} and \textit{how} questions are relevant for understanding overall model behaviour, which interests experts and developers \parencite{Miller2019}. In contrast, users of \ac{XAI} systems are often more interested in answers to \textit{why} questions that provide sufficient causes for a system's output \parencite{Kelley1987}. The rationale for this is that the computation and comprehension of full causal chains requires significant cognitive effort \parencite{Lipton1990}. What counts as a sufficient cause for an outcome depends on the explainee's subjective perception, shaped by their prior knowledge and beliefs \parencite{Kashima1998}, which will be discussed further in Section~\ref{sssec:theory-of-mind-ai}.

\subsubsection{Generative vs. Discriminative Models} \label{sssec:generative-vs-discriminative}

In this thesis, the distinction between \textit{generative} and \textit{discriminative} models is particularly important, as it has significant implications for model capabilities and interactions \parencite{Schneider2024}. These two classes of models differ fundamentally in their modelling objectives and outputs, which in turn shapes the \ac{XAI} design and the nature of explanations needed to understand them \parencite{Kumarage2026}.

\textit{Discriminative} models are trained to distinguish between two or more classes. Conceptually, these models learn to approximate the mapping from input features to output labels \parencite{Kumarage2026}. Classic use cases are image classification \parencite[e.g.,][]{Lu2007}, anomaly detection \parencite[e.g.,][]{Edozie2025} or sentiment analysis \parencite[e.g.,][]{Wankhade2022}. Depending on the specific application, this mapping can be implemented using simple linear regression models, decision trees, but also more complex models such as support vector machines or deep neural networks \parencite{Schneider2024}. \textit{Discriminative} models have a limited output size, with at most a few hundred bits of information in an information-theoretic sense \parencite{Schneider2024}. The output is also often deterministic, and all possible outputs are known at training time \parencite{Kumarage2026}.

By contrast, \textit{generative} models are trained to learn the underlying data distribution of a given dataset and to generate new samples that are similar to the training data \parencite{Banh2023}. Applications like text generation \parencite[e.g.,][]{Brown2020} and image synthesis \parencite[e.g.,][]{Rombach2022} are common use cases for generative models. Enabled by advancements in deep learning, such as transformer-based architectures, generative models have become increasingly powerful and versatile in recent years \parencite[e.g.,][]{OpenAI2025a}. Because of their open-ended nature, generative model outputs are virtually unlimited in size but often contain tens of thousands of bits of information \parencite{Schneider2024}. The outputs are also often non-deterministic \parencite{Kumarage2026}.

These differences in task and output characteristics between \textit{generative} and \textit{discriminative} models have significant implications for \ac{XAI}. Explainability approaches for discriminative models are often built on the assumption that model inputs can be mapped to outputs deterministically. This enables mathematical techniques such as feature attribution, counterfactual explanations, or perturbation-based methods \parencite{Kumarage2026} that attempt to assign causal responsibility to input features. Generative models break this assumption of determinism, and their outputs are determined by a series of stochastic sampling steps \parencite{Kumarage2026}. The resulting behaviour of generative models is therefore encoded in abstract latent representations that do not map cleanly to input features, uncoupling it from the inputs \parencite{Schneider2024}.

Practically, this means \ac{XAI} methods can be applied to \textit{generative} \ac{AI} models, but the resulting explanations are often unreliable \parencite{Kumarage2026}. This highlights a paradigm shift in explainability between \textit{generative} and \textit{discriminative} models. This paradigm shift requires new methods for generating explanations. Attempts to adopt existing methods like \acs{LIME} or \acs{SHAP} have been made and will be discussed in the following section alongside newer approaches to explainability in Section~\ref{sssec:xai-methods}. However, the fundamental differences between the two classes of models suggest that a more fruitful approach to \ac{XAI} for generative models is to develop methods that are specifically designed for their unique characteristics \parencite{Schneider2024}.

\subsubsection{Explainability Methods} \label{sssec:xai-methods}

Explainability methods are techniques designed to provide details about the functioning and reasons for the behaviour of machine learning models as defined in Section~\ref{sssec:terminology}. For less complex models, such as linear regression or decision trees, the model itself can be directly inspected to understand its behaviour (i.e., \textit{transparency}) \parencite{Molnar2025}. In contrast, more complex models, such as deep neural networks, are often considered \textit{black-box} models \parencite{Lipton2018}, which require explanations through surrogate models or other proxy techniques to understand \parencite{Retzlaff2024}. The distinction between \textit{transparent} and \textit{black-box} models correlates with the timing of explanation generation, as methods used to explain black-box models are often categorised as \textit{post-hoc}, as they generate explanations after the fact \parencite{Retzlaff2024}. Beyond providing reasons for a model's behaviour, explainability methods can also be categorised by the scope of the explanation and the number of models they can be applied to \parencite{Ali2023}. Models can be explained either on a \textit{local} per-output basis, explaining a specific decision or output or on a \textit{global} level describing the overall behaviour of the model \parencite{Rai2020,Ali2023}.  Regarding the number of models, \textit{model-specific} methods are designed for a specific model or model architecture and assume certain properties of the model to generate explanations, while \textit{model-agnostic} methods can be applied to any model type without assumptions about its internal structure \parencite{Ali2023}.

The rest of this section describes three explainability methods particularly relevant to the research in this thesis: \acs{LIME}, \acs{SHAP}, and \ac{CoT} explanations. \acs{LIME} and \acs{SHAP} are included because they are widely used and have been adopted to explain generative models, while \ac{CoT} explanations are included because they are unique to \acp{LLM} and can be implemented with minimal technical overhead.

\paragraph*{LIME (\acl{LIME})}

\acs{LIME} is a model-agnostic method designed to explain individual predictions (i.e., explain locally) \parencite{Ribeiro2016}. The core mechanism of \acs{LIME} is a local approximation of the target model's behaviour around the input instance whose prediction is being explained. Explanations are generated by creating a set of perturbed samples around the instance of interest (e.g., by changing or removing features) and observing the changes in the target model's predictions \parencite{Ribeiro2016}. An interpretable surrogate model, such as linear regression, is then fitted to this perturbed data. The surrogate model provides a local explanation by highlighting which input features could have influenced the specific prediction. An example of \acs{LIME}'s perturbation and surrogate model fitting process is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:lime-perturbations}.

The perturbed samples (light red cross and blue dots) are scaled by their proximity to the instance being explained. \textit{Reused from \textcite{Ribeiro2016}}.}
    \label{fig:lime-perturbations}

\acs{LIME}'s free choice of surrogate model and perturbation strategy allows it to be applied to virtually any model \parencite{Ribeiro2016}. The computational requirements of \acs{LIME} are also relatively low, as it only requires a limited number of perturbations and a simple surrogate model to generate explanations \parencite{Ribeiro2016}. However, the quality of \acs{LIME} explanations is sensitive to the choice of perturbation strategy \parencite{Ribeiro2016}. Combined with the local nature of the explanations, this can lead to instability in the explanations generated \parencite{Alvarez2018}. Additionally, the surrogate model may not correctly capture the behaviour of the original model, leading to explanations that are not faithful to the model's actual decision-making process \parencite{Miller2019}. Applying \acs{LIME} to generative models is challenging, as the simplicity of surrogate models does not allow them to capture high-level concepts \parencite{Kumarage2026}. Despite this, the general concept of \acs{LIME} has seen adoption in larger frameworks for \ac{GenAI} explanation \parencite[e.g.,][]{Ahmed2025, Durango2025}.

\paragraph*{SHAP (\acl{SHAP})}

\acs{SHAP} is a model-agnostic method that functions through additive feature attributions \parencite{Lundberg2017}. In principle, \acs{SHAP} calculates how important each input feature is for the model's output. For this purpose, the method uses Shapley values from cooperative game theory, which provide a way to \enquote{fairly} distribute a payout (i.e., model output) among players based on their contribution (importance) to the total payout \parencite{Shapley1953}. To do this, \acs{SHAP} evaluates the feature's contribution across all possible subsets of features by comparing how the model's output when a feature is included versus excluded, then averages these contributions to determine overall feature importance \parencite{Lundberg2017,Shapley1953}. The method can be used to produce local or global explanations, depending on whether the feature attributions are calculated for a specific instance or averaged across multiple instances \parencite{Lundberg2017}. Local explanations can be visualised using force plots, such as the example in Figure~\ref{fig:shap-force-plot}, showing a local explanation for the decision to reject a loan application.

    \caption[\acs{SHAP} force plot]{A force plot visualising the local feature attributions calculated by \acs{SHAP} for a rejected loan application. Features are split into positively contributing features (in red on the left) and negatively contributing features (in blue on the right). The size of the bars indicates the magnitude of the contribution. \textit{Reused from \textcite{Martens2025}}.}
    \label{fig:shap-force-plot}

\acs{SHAP}'s robust theoretical foundation provides a strong basis for its explanations, yielding consistent and theoretically sound feature attributions \parencite{Alvarez2018}. Force plots allow intuitive visualisations that non-experts can understand \parencite{Martens2025}. However, the computational cost of \acs{SHAP} scales exponentially with the number of features, making it impractical for models with many input features, like \acp{LLM} \parencite{Lundberg2017}. Additionally, \acs{SHAP}'s assumption of feature independence can lead to incorrect explanations in cases where features are correlated, which is often the case in real-world data \parencite{Aas2021}. Like \acs{LIME}, applying \acs{SHAP} to generative models is challenging because its reliance on feature attributions does not align well with the abstract latent representations used by generative models \parencite{Kumarage2026}. Still, \acs{SHAP} has seen adoption in combination with partial dependence plots to explain generative models through proxies \parencite{Wang2023}.

\paragraph*{Chain-of-Thought Explanations}

\ac{CoT} is an approach to interpretability that is specific to \acp{LLM}. The core idea is to have the model follow a step-by-step reasoning process that leads to the final answer \parencite{Wei2022}. These intermediate steps can then serve as explanations for the model's final answer, in the sense of the explanation provided in Section~\ref{sssec:terminology}, because they provide detail about the model's reasoning process. The step-by-step reasoning can be elicited through training \parencite{Kim2023} or even zero-shot prompting \parencite{Kojima2022}, making it a method that can be implemented with minimal technical overhead. A central criticism of \ac{CoT} explanations is that they may not faithfully reflect the model's actual internal reasoning process \parencite{Lyu2023}. Since the model generates its own explanations, these explanations inherit the same biases and limitations as the model's outputs \parencite{Lyu2023}. Despite these limitations, \ac{CoT} explanations have shown promise in improving performance on complex tasks by encouraging systematic problem decomposition \parencite{Wei2022}, and they provide a unique avenue for gaining insight into the reasoning processes of \acp{LLM}.

\subsection{Cognitive Psychology} \label{ssec:cognitive-psychology}

This section introduces the concepts from cognitive psychology that this thesis builds on. Section~\ref{sssec:cognitive-psychology-concepts} covers dual-process theory, heuristics, and mental models, the primary construct of this thesis. \ac{ToM} is introduced in Section~\ref{sssec:theory-of-mind}. Section~\ref{sssec:theory-of-mind-ai} turns to \ac{ToM} in the context of \ac{AI}, where it is adopted as an account of how users form and revise their mental models.

\subsubsection{Relevant Concepts from Cognitive Psychology} \label{sssec:cognitive-psychology-concepts}

Before introducing \ac{ToM}, this section establishes three concepts from cognitive psychology that this thesis builds on. \textit{Dual-process theory} and \textit{heuristics} describe how people process information to form judgements and make decisions \parencite[cf.][]{Jussupow2021}. Building on these concepts, the section closes on a definition of \textit{mental models}, which are the primary construct of this thesis.

\textit{Dual-process theory} is a group of theories that propose the existence of two distinct types of cognitive processing in humans \parencite{Evans2013}, generically labelled as \textit{Type 1} and \textit{Type 2} processing. Different theories ascribe different characteristics to these two types of processing, but they generally agree on the distinction between an intuitive, automatic \textit{Type 1} processing and a more deliberate, controlled \textit{Type 2} processing \parencite{Kahneman2011}. Beyond these common characteristics, different theories ascribe different additional characteristics to the two types of processing, such as speed, parallelism, consciousness, and response bias \parencite{Evans2013}. Table~\ref{tab:type1-type2-processing} summarises the common characteristics alongside a selection of different characteristics supported by several theories but not universally agreed upon.

Within dual-process theory, different relationships between the two types of processing are proposed. Default-interventionist accounts propose that \textit{Type 1} processing generates an initial response that \textit{Type 2} may subsequently override \parencite{Evans2013}. Parallel-competitive accounts, on the other hand, assume that both processes can operate in parallel and that conflicts are resolved between them if they arise \parencite{Evans2013}. For this thesis, both accounts are acknowledged, but the default-interventionist perspective is the primary working assumption, as empirical evidence better supports it \parencite{Evans2013, Kahneman2011}. Additionally, the parallel-competitive account would require the fast \textit{Type 1} to wait for the slower \textit{Type 2} processing to resolve conflicts, which would eliminate the speed advantage of \textit{Type 1} processing \parencite{Evans2013}.

\textit{Heuristics} are problem-solving methods that simplify a problem to find a solution faster, often at the expense of optimality. The core mechanism of these mental shortcuts is to ignore part of the information available for finding an optimal solution \parencite{Gigerenzer2011}. Research on heuristics traces back to the theory of bounded rationality, which states that human decision-making is characterised by limited time, mental resources, and information \parencite{Simon1956}. Despite these limitations, humans were able to make decisions that yielded satisfactory results, leading \textcite{Simon1956} to coin the term \enquote{satisficing}; a portmanteau of \enquote{satisfy} and \enquote{suffice}. It describes the behavioural pattern of choosing the first acceptable solution rather than searching for an optimal one \parencite{Hjeij2023}. Heuristics also play an important role in dual-process theory, as they are often associated with \textit{Type 1} processing \parencite{Kahneman2011}. In computer science and \ac{IS} research, heuristics are frequently used to solve problems that would otherwise be too complex to solve \parencite{Hjeij2023}. A classic example of this is the travelling salesman problem, to which a good solution can be found quite efficiently. An optimal solution can be found, but the computational requirements would be prohibitive for large instances of the problem \parencite{Hjeij2023}.

\textcite{Norman1983} defines \textit{mental models} in comparison to three distinct \enquote{things}. The \textit{target system} is the system being modelled. The \textit{conceptual model} is an accurate and complete representation of the target system. Through use, a user develops and revises their \textit{mental model}, yielding a personal representation of the target system, limited by the user's prior knowledge and experience with similar systems. Because mental models are not directly observable, researchers reconstruct the \textit{scientist's conceptualisation} of users' mental models \parencite{Norman1983}. The mental models formed by users are incomplete, often inaccurate, and constructed to be processed with the least cognitive effort \parencite{Norman1983}. This parsimony suggests that users' mental models are subject to a form of satisficing, where users strive to form a mental model sufficient to achieve their goals. The following sections introduce \ac{ToM} in detail (Section~\ref{sssec:theory-of-mind}) and propose \ac{ToM} application as an account of how users form and revise their mental models of \ac{AI} systems (Section~\ref{sssec:theory-of-mind-ai}).

\subsubsection{Theory of Mind} \label{sssec:theory-of-mind}

\ac{ToM} refers to the cognitive capability to attribute mental states to oneself and others and to understand that others have beliefs, desires, and intentions that may differ from one's own \parencite{Wellman2014}. The foundational work on \ac{ToM} originates from research by \textcite{Premack1978}, who investigated whether chimpanzees possess the ability to infer mental states of others. Today's research on \ac{ToM} in humans investigates a large variety of phenomena, such as the development of \ac{ToM} in children \parencite{Wellman2014}, factors affecting \ac{ToM} performance in adults \parencite{Apperly2009}, and the role of \ac{ToM} in psychological disorders such as autism spectrum disorder \parencite{BaronCohen1995}.

The core of \ac{ToM} is the understanding that individuals hold subjective mental representations of the world and act according to these representations. Literature on \ac{ToM} often focuses on three primary components that form the basis of \ac{ToM}: \textit{beliefs}, \textit{desires}, and \textit{intentions} (BDI) \parencite{Wellman2014}. \textit{Beliefs} represent fundamental facts about the world as perceived by an individual. \textit{Desires} reflect an individual's subjective goals for what they want the world to look like. \textit{Intentions} represent planned future actions aimed at satisfying \textit{desires} given the individual's \textit{beliefs}. These components are deeply interrelated and form the basis for \textit{belief reasoning} (or \textit{belief-desire reasoning}), which is the ability to make rational inferences about others' actions based on their \textit{beliefs} and \textit{desires} \parencite{Wellman2014, Rakoczy2022}. For instance, knowing another person's \textit{beliefs} and \textit{desires} allows one to infer their likely \textit{intentions} or actions to satisfy those \textit{desires}. A complete example of BDI is shown in Table~\ref{tab:bdi-example}. Some researchers have proposed additional components that act as moderators of the BDI components, such as \textit{emotions} and \textit{thoughts} \parencite{Apperly2009}. To keep the discussion concise, this thesis focuses on the BDI components as the core of \ac{ToM}, while acknowledging that other components may also play a role in \ac{ToM} reasoning.

The general capability enabling \ac{ToM} is called \textit{meta-representation}, which is the understanding that others perceive and represent the world from their own subjective point of view \parencite{Perner1993}. This meta-representational understanding of others is characterised by four distinct signatures, which are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:tom-signatures}. \textit{Difference} refers to the understanding that others' perceptions may differ from one's own, opening the possibility for different perspectives. \textit{Incompatibility} describes the notion that these different perspectives may be fundamentally incompatible. \textit{Misrepresentation} captures the understanding that others' representations may be inaccurate or incorrect relative to reality. Finally, \textit{aspectuality} reflects the view that others' representations may consider only certain aspects of reality while ignoring others \parencite{Rakoczy2022}.

The signature of \textit{misrepresentation} is of particular interest for \ac{ToM} research, as it is fundamental to the notion of \textit{false beliefs}. The ability to understand that others can hold beliefs that are false relative to reality has long been considered a key benchmark for \ac{ToM} competence in children \parencite{Wellman2001}. The understanding of \textit{false beliefs} is most often tested using scenarios similar to the Wimmer-Perner task \parencite{Wimmer1983}, which is a four-step scenario involving a character (Maxi):

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item Maxi places chocolate in a cupboard and leaves the room.
    \item While Maxi is absent, the chocolate is moved to a drawer.
    \item Maxi returns to the room.
    \item The child is asked where Maxi will look for the chocolate.
\end{enumerate}

When asked where Maxi will look for the chocolate upon returning, three-year-olds typically answer incorrectly (the drawer), while four- and five-year-olds answer correctly approximately 60\% of the time, with accuracy improving to 86\% in older children \parencite{Wimmer1983}. Despite this, \textcite{Wellman2001} argue that false belief is a benchmark to use cautiously for determining \ac{ToM} competence, especially in young children: other factors, such as the experimental setting and individual differences among participants, can influence performance on \textit{false-belief} tasks. Nonetheless, understanding \textit{false belief} represents a significant milestone in the development of \ac{ToM} in children, as it demonstrates the ability to understand that others can hold beliefs that are not only different from one's own but also incorrect relative to reality \parencite{Wellman2014}.

\textcite{Apperly2009} propose a \enquote{two-systems} account of belief reasoning, which posits the existence of two distinct cognitive systems for belief reasoning. The first system is efficient but inflexible, while the second system is flexible but cognitively demanding. They draw parallels to other areas of cognition like number perception, where adults are capable of quickly comparing two numbers or group sizes with one another without making a conscious effort \parencite{Apperly2009}. This parallels dual-process theories of cognition, where the first system is associated with Type 1 processing and the second system with Type 2 processing.

\subsubsection{Theory of Mind in \acs{AI}} \label{sssec:theory-of-mind-ai}

The intersection of \ac{ToM} and \ac{AI} can be categorised into two research directions. The first direction focuses on equipping \ac{AI} systems with \ac{ToM}-like capabilities. \textcite{Baker2017} introduced the \ac{BToM} framework, which helps \ac{AI} systems to infer mental states (beliefs, desires) based on an agent's behaviour. The primary challenge of this approach is the computational complexity of inferring mental states through Bayesian inference. More recently, the idea of spontaneous \ac{ToM} emergence in large language models has gained attention as a result of training on human-generated text. GPT-4 was able to solve 75\% of false-belief tasks, suggesting some level of \ac{ToM} capability \parencite{Kosinski2024}. However, subsequent studies have shown that this performance drastically decreases even with minor perturbations to the task \parencite{Chen2024, Ullman2023}. This suggests that the model's performance may be driven by surface-level heuristics rather than genuine mentalising capabilities \parencite[\textit{cf.}][]{Aru2023}.

The second research direction focuses on the application of \ac{ToM} to \ac{AI} systems regardless of whether \ac{AI} systems actually have beliefs, desires, or intentions. Users ascribe knowledge and intent to AI systems, which shapes their engagement with them \parencite{Wang2021}. Inaccurate mental models of \ac{AI} systems can lead to overreliance on or underutilisation of these systems, which can degrade task performance \parencite{Jussupow2021}. Additionally, the attribution of mental states to \acp{LLM} predicts users' willingness to rely on their recommendations in decision-making \parencite{Colombatto2025}.

\textcite{Heider1944} showed that the attribution of mental states is applicable to non-human agents as well. They demonstrated that humans spontaneously attribute mental states to geometric shapes based on their movement patterns. This disposition can be extended to autonomous intelligent systems \parencite{deGraaf2017}, with people applying the same folk-psychological concepts they use to explain human behaviour to explain the behaviour of artificial agents \parencite[cf.][]{Miller2019}. Given this disposition, this thesis uses \ac{ToM} as an account of how users form and revise their mental models of \ac{AI} systems, and adopts that relation as a working assumption for the analysis that follows. Attributions of mental capacities, such as what a system can perceive, know, or reason about, are treated as part of the attribution of mental states \parencite[cf.][]{Colombatto2025}. Because mental models are not directly observable (cf. Section~\ref{sssec:cognitive-psychology-concepts}), this thesis relies on this account to infer users' mental models from the mental states they attribute.

\ac{MToM} is an emerging framework that integrates both research directions and highlights their complementary nature. In \ac{MToM}, both humans and \ac{AI} construct and revise models of each other through interaction \parencite{Wang2021, Wang2024a}. The bidirectional nature of \ac{MToM} provides an approach for closing the mental model gap between humans (particularly lay users) and even \ac{XAI} systems \parencite{Bienefeld2023}. As explanation is a social process \parencite{Miller2019}, the design and selection of explanations in \ac{XAI} has to be tailored to the explainee. This tailoring requires understanding the explainee's prior knowledge and beliefs \parencite{Halpern2005}. Conversely, useful explanations can help users form accurate mental models of \ac{AI} systems and increase trust in them \parencite{Shin2021}. This positions \ac{XAI} as a key mechanism for supporting accurate mental models while highlighting the importance of understanding how humans apply \ac{ToM} when designing effective \ac{AI} explanations \parencite{Hoffman2023}.

\subsection{Related Works} \label{ssec:related-works}

The rising interest in \ac{XAI} for generative models and the intersection of mental models and \ac{AI} has led to a growing body of research in these areas. This section provides an overview of the works most relevant to the research conducted in this thesis. \textcite{Weisz2025} developed six design principles for \ac{GenAI}, each with four strategies, to support more effective design. As generative \ac{AI} fundamentally shifts the user's role in the interaction from \textit{command} (specifying exact actions) to \textit{specification} (specifying desired outcomes), existing design principles and \ac{HCI} frameworks are not suited to guide the design of \ac{GenAI} systems \parencite{Weisz2025}. The authors also developed a new design vocabulary alongside the design principles, as the existing vocabulary developed for discriminative \ac{AI} is not sufficient to discuss the unique characteristics of generative \ac{AI} \parencite{Weisz2025}. The chapter was developed and validated through an iterative process that combined literature review, practitioner workshops, and the examination of live generative AI products \parencite{Weisz2025}.

Two design principles are particularly relevant to this thesis, as they focus on users' mental models and their trust in and reliance on the system. \textit{Principle 2: Design for Mental Models} states the importance of designing generative \ac{AI} systems in a way that supports users in forming a \enquote{useful} mental model of the system, which is defined as a mental model that allows users to predict the system's behaviour and understand its limitations \parencite{Weisz2025}. This definition mirrors Norman's (\citeyear{Norman1983}) concept of a \enquote{workable} model. Specific strategies include clarifying generative variability to the user and providing the model with knowledge about the user and their context \parencite{Weisz2025}. \textit{Principle 3: Design for Appropriate Trust and Reliance} suggests strategies such as providing users with clear information about the system's capabilities and limitations, as well as providing users with \enquote{rationales for outputs}. Additionally, the user should be discouraged from overreliance on the system by introducing friction during key decision-making points \parencite{Weisz2025}. Because these design principles are developed for generative \ac{AI} in general, they are highly abstract; applying them to specific use cases requires further research.

\textcite{Jussupow2021} performed an experiment to investigate how doctors evaluate and follow or reject diagnoses from a \ac{CAID} system. The experiment was conducted in a simulated environment and with a \ac{CAID} system which provided both correct and incorrect diagnoses. The results showed that doctors could consistently reject incorrect diagnoses and accept correct ones only when they accurately estimated the system's capabilities \parencite{Jussupow2021}. Novice doctors were more likely to overestimate the system's capabilities, leading to more type I errors, while experienced doctors were more likely to underestimate them, leading to more type II errors \parencite{Jussupow2021}.

The authors find that doctors' mental models of the system significantly influenced how they evaluated and used the system's diagnoses \parencite{Jussupow2021}. Critically, doctors underwent two key cognitive conflicts when working with conflicting \ac{AI} advice compared to their own assessment:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Belief Conflict:} The doctor compares their own belief about the system's capabilities to their belief in their own capabilities to make the right diagnosis. If one belief is stronger, doctors often immediately accept or reject the system's diagnosis without further reflection.
    \item \textit{Validation Conflict:} If no belief is stronger, doctors move to validate their own diagnosis and the system's diagnosis. They search for evidence that supports either diagnosis in a conscious effort steered by metacognitions about the reliability of the system and their own capabilities.
\end{enumerate}

This has significant implications for \ac{AI}-supported decision-making, as useful mental models are not only helpful for improving performance \parencite{Weisz2025}, but also a precondition for effective human-AI collaboration \parencite{Jussupow2021}. The study also highlights the importance of metacognition and ongoing reflection on \ac{AI} systems' capabilities and limitations for successful human-AI collaboration \parencite{Jussupow2021}.

Focusing on goal communication, \textcite{Ying2024} investigated how communicating the \ac{AI}'s belief state to users affects their acceptance of the \ac{AI}'s suggestions. In an online experiment with 210 participants, an \ac{AI} observed users' ingredient selection during shopping to infer their intended recipe and provide suggestions based on this inference. They tested three conditions in a within-subject design:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\arabic*)]
    \item \textit{Baseline:} The \ac{AI} withheld the inferred recipe from the user, only providing suggestions based on its inference.
    \item \textit{Tell:} The \ac{AI} communicated a confidence score for its inferred recipe.
    \item \textit{Show:} The \ac{AI} communicated the inferred recipe itself to the user using modal language according to the certainty.
\end{enumerate}

Their results showed that users were more likely to accept suggestions from the \ac{AI} when it communicated its belief state (i.e., in the \textit{Tell} and \textit{Show} conditions) compared to the \textit{baseline}. Additionally, the two conditions that communicated the belief state received higher subjective ratings for willingness to use and trust, as well as perceived helpfulness \parencite{Ying2024}. While the quantitative results showed no significant differences between the two modes, qualitative data suggest that users preferred \textit{Tell} to \textit{Show}, although the latter yielded higher trust calibration.

Similarly, \textcite{Colombatto2025} investigated how mental state attributions influence user trust in \acp{LLM}. In an online experiment with 410 participants, they tested the correlation of mental state attributions to ChatGPT with the willingness to rely on its recommendations in decision-making. In the experiment, participants answered general knowledge questions with two response options and could change their answer after receiving a recommendation from an \ac{LLM}. Participants also completed a questionnaire measuring their mental state attributions to \acp{LLM} in two key dimensions: \textit{intelligence} (e.g., memory, planning) and \textit{experience} (e.g., happiness, resentment). The results showed that the two dimensions of mental state attributions affected willingness to rely on the \ac{LLM}'s recommendations differently. Higher attributions of intelligence were associated with a higher willingness to rely on the \ac{LLM}'s recommendations. Conversely, higher attributions of experience were associated with a lower willingness to rely on the \ac{LLM}'s recommendations \parencite{Colombatto2025}. The authors also point out a notable difference between self-reported trust and actual reliance on the \ac{LLM}'s recommendations, with the latter more strongly correlated with advice accuracy than with perceived anthropomorphism \parencite{Colombatto2025}.

\subsection{Research Objectives} \label{ssec:research-objectives}

The state of research presented in the previous sections highlights the importance of mental models for effective human-AI collaboration \parencite[cf.][]{Jussupow2021}. Due to the nascent nature of the field, there are still significant gaps in understanding how users form mental models of \ac{GenAI} systems. \textcite{Jussupow2021} explicitly identify the role of explanations as a topic of future research, pointing to a direct link between \ac{XAI} and the formation of accurate mental models. Additionally, while design principles for \ac{GenAI} have been developed, their application to specific use cases requires further investigation \parencite{Weisz2025}. This thesis addresses these gaps by investigating the role of explanations in forming accurate mental models of \ac{LLM}-based systems. To this end, the thesis is guided by the following research questions:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\textbf{RQ\arabic*}),leftmargin=4em]
    \item Which mental states do \ac{LLM}-users attribute to \acp{LLM} in collaborative problem-solving scenarios, and what do these attributions reveal about their mental model of the system?
    \item Does providing explanations for \ac{LLM} outputs influence users' attribution of mental states, and if so, in what direction and on which dimensions of attribution?
    \item Based on the findings of \textbf{RQ1} and \textbf{RQ2}, what design principles can be derived for explainable \ac{LLM}-based systems to support accurate mental models?
\end{enumerate}

The central objective of this thesis is to develop a set of empirically grounded design principles for explainable \ac{LLM}-based systems that support the formation of accurate mental models. This objective is pursued by evaluating a pre-existing user study, re-examined through the analytic lens of \ac{ToM}, to investigate how explanations affect users' attribution of mental states to an \ac{LLM} in a collaborative problem-solving scenario. This evaluation's findings are used to derive design principles for \ac{LLM}-based systems, which are then evaluated and refined through expert interviews.
