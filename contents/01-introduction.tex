\section{Introduction} \label{sec:introduction}

The emergence of \ac{GenAI} has created unprecedented interest in \ac{AI}. This public interest has fuelled a surge in research and development, leading to the rapid advancement of \ac{GenAI} technologies. In particular, advances in \acp{LLM} have enabled the creation and widespread adoption of chatbots such as ChatGPT \parencite{OpenAI2025a} or specialised tools like Claude Code \parencite{AnthropicInc2026}. Due to the rapid development of technology, research in the fields of \ac{HAII} and \ac{XAI} has fallen behind \parencite{Adadi2018}.

Mental models play a significant role in shaping how humans interact with \ac{AI} systems. They influence how users perceive these systems, interpret their outputs, and rely on \ac{AI} \parencite{Miller2019,Bodamer2026}. Mental models are subject to change over time, particularly as users gain experience with \ac{AI} systems \parencite{Schneider2025}. Anthropomorphism also plays an important role in forming mental models: users may attribute human-like qualities to \ac{AI} systems \parencite{Bodamer2026}.

Explainability is crucial for building trust in and acceptance of \ac{AI} systems \parencite{Arrieta2020}. Established methods such as \acs{LIME} \parencite{Ribeiro2016} and \acs{SHAP} \parencite{Lundberg2017} have been developed and validated to provide explanations for discriminative models. These methods, however, are not directly applicable to \ac{GenAI} systems, for which no established methods exist. In addition, \textcite{Bienefeld2023} have shown that, even for discriminative models, many explanation methods are too technical to be effective for lay users.

The growing adoption of \ac{GenAI} in various domains, including healthcare and education, highlights the need for measures that enable effective and responsible use of these systems. Recent research has shown that mental models have an impact on task performance in human-AI collaboration settings \parencite{Jussupow2021}. Only if users have an accurate mental model of the \ac{AI} system can they effectively collaborate with it. Due to the nascent state of the field, two key areas remain underexplored \parencite{Bodamer2026,Weisz2025}:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Understanding how users form and update their mental models of explainable \ac{LLM}-based systems, especially in the context of collaborative problem-solving tasks.
    \item 1. Limited research on the explainability of \ac{LLM}-based systems has led to a lack of design guidelines to support accurate mental models in human-\ac{LLM} collaboration.
\end{enumerate}

This thesis addresses these gaps by analysing data from a user study in which participants collaborated with an explainable \ac{LLM}-based system to solve maths problems. From the analysis, design principles for explainable \ac{LLM}-based systems are derived. The rest of this thesis is structured as follows: The fundamental concepts and related works from the fields of \ac{XAI}, \ac{GenAI} and cognitive psychology are explained in Chapter~\ref{sec:fundamentals}. This chapter presents the research questions at the end. Chapter~\ref{sec:methodology} explains \ac{DSR} as the research methodology and describes the specific research methods employed in this thesis. Chapter~\ref{sec:problem-awareness} presents a literature review on current challenges in the field of \ac{HAII}, highlighting current research on explanations for \ac{AI} systems and their impact on users' mental models. The user study evaluated in this thesis is described in Chapter~\ref{sec:artefact-design}, with particular focus on the chatbots created for the study. Chapter~\ref{sec:design-principles} presents most of the analytic work, discussing the analysis procedure, the derivation of the design principles, and their evaluation. Chapter~\ref{sec:discussion} synthesises the findings and discusses their implications in the broader research context. The chapter concludes by outlining the thesis's limitations and providing directions for future research. Finally, Chapter~\ref{sec:conclusion} closes the thesis with a summary of the findings.
