\section*{Abstract}

Mental models shape how users perceive and interact with AI systems and are central to enabling effective human-AI collaboration. Explanations can help users form accurate mental models, but the paradigm shift to generative AI (GenAI) has rendered established explanation methods largely inapplicable. This thesis investigates how users form and update mental models of explainable LLM-based systems during collaborative problem-solving and proposes design principles for such systems to support accurate mental models.

This thesis analyses a user study conducted prior to this thesis using a grounded theory approach to derive the design principles. In the study, twelve participants solved maths problems with the assistance of an explainable LLM-based chatbot, with one group receiving explanations and the other not. The think-aloud protocols are coded in two cycles and assembled into a data structure showing how participants interact with the system. The data structure is analysed through the lens of Theory of Mind (ToM) attribution to derive the design principles. The design principles are refined through feedback from expert interviews.

The analysis shows that attributions of mental states are spread across a cyclical interaction pattern and focus on the system's knowledge and capabilities. Collaboration issues arise when participants misattribute these constructs. The resulting design theory is evaluated positively, but leaves a part of the design space empirically uncovered. Theory-derived design principles fill this gap. The thesis contributes by proposing a methodology for investigating users' mental models and new design knowledge, which future research can validate.
